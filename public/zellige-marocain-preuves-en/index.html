Moroccan Zellij: historical, textual and material evidence

A documentary dossier based on early texts, dated monuments, technical analysis and the continuity of a living craft tradition in Morocco.

Author: Ould Sanhaja
DOCUMENTARY SYNTHESISThe dossier rests on a chain of converging evidence: texts, monuments, techniques, archives and living craft transmission.

Executive summary
Central questionWhat do historical, material and technical sources show about the antiquity and continuity of zellij in Morocco?
Main findingThe sources converge on a long-documented Moroccan tradition, especially visible in Fez, Marrakesh, Meknes and Ceuta.
MethodDistinguish glazed ceramics in general from cut-and-assembled zellij, then compare texts, monuments, processes and craft transmission.

Study

From bejmat to cut zellij: a documented technical evolution

Al-Qarawiyyin in 526 H / 1131

Ibn Abi Zar al-Fasi records the repaving of al-Qarawiyyin's courtyard in Rawd al-Qirtas. He names the master Muhammad b. Ahmad b. Muhammad al-Khawlani and mentions the manufacture of “brick resembling bejmat”, together with a detailed account of levelling and water drainage. The text also states that an earlier pavement had existed.

Significance: this is early written evidence for ceramic paving in Fez before Marinid maturity.

Functional evolution

A cautious sequence can be proposed: large paving units / bejmat → wider glazed-tile use → zellij in floors and wall dados → small cut pieces assembled geometrically in the Marinid period. Al-Umari's testimony is central because he describes zellij in Fez floors and wall dados.

Ceuta in 825 H

Muhammad b. al-Qasim al-Subti explicitly describes a basin “paved with coloured zellij”, providing direct Moroccan textual evidence from the early fifteenth century.

View source →

Qal'a of the Beni Hammad: compare techniques, not labels

Museum With No Frontiers describes a Hammadid panel made of moulded, enamelled and lustre-painted ceramic; its stars and crosses are about 21 cm in diameter. These are large moulded elements, not necessarily small units created by post-firing cutting.

By contrast, the ArchéoSciences study of fourteenth-century Moroccan zellij describes a process in which tiles are glazed and fired, then cut and finally assembled into geometric panels.

Fez raw material: what science actually establishes

A recent study of Fez clays shows that potters traditionally use two clay types from the Benjlik site, fine and sandy clays from Miocene deposits in the Fez basin. Their mineralogical and chemical composition and their ceramic suitability have been scientifically characterized.

Supported claim: Fez has a scientifically studied local raw material traditionally tied to pottery and zellij production.

Tlemcen: historical and technical comparison

Methodological note : Modern borders inherited from colonial rule should not be projected onto earlier centuries. Attribution should be based on chronology, patronage, technique, written sources and the political context of each monument.

From Moroccan craftsmen to Marinid influence on Nasrid architecture

Historical sources and archaeological studies show that relations between Morocco and al-Andalus involved real movements of craftsmen, master builders and technical knowledge.

For the construction of Seville's great mosque in 567 H / 1172, Ibn Sahib al-Salat records the participation of master builders from Marrakesh, Fez and the Maghribi shore alongside Andalusi craftsmen.

In the sixteenth century, Luis del Mármol Carvajal states that the rulers of Granada acted « a imitación de los reyes de Fez » and adds that « los reyes de Granada siempre fueron imitando a los de Fez ».

Antonio Malpica Cuello points to « interesantes paralelos » between Marinid and Nasrid ceramics and describes them as « estrechamente ligadas ». Research by the Patronato de la Alhambra further notes that a precise ceramic-inlay technique is documented, as far as currently known, only in Nasrid and Marinid art.

Conclusion: a documented line can be traced from Almohad-era movement of Moroccan craftsmen to Fez/Marinid influence in Granada and direct technical affinities between Marinid and Nasrid ceramics. For zellij specifically, this establishes a strong historical and technical environment for transfer and influence without proving a one-way transfer of every technique.

Moroccan continuity: from the Almohads to a living craft

12th c.Documented Almohad stage at the Koutoubia and Kasbah Mosque in Marrakech.
14th c.High technical maturity in Marinid Fez, including Bou Inania (1351–1356).
16th c.Saadian continuity, including El Badi Palace in Marrakech, begun in 1578.
Later periodsArchitectural and artisanal continuity through the Alaouite period and in workshops of Fez, Meknes and Marrakech to the present.
Core point: the strongest argument is not archaeological priority alone but a continuous historical and artisanal chain: master, apprentice, workshop, kiln, glazing, cutting, tools, geometric calculation, assembly and transmission.

Independent external documentation before the modern dispute

Albert Kahn archive — Fez and Meknes, 1913

Photographs of urban spaces and fountains document zellij in everyday city life, not merely as a museum object, long before current heritage disputes.

Paris Colonial Exhibition, 1931

If the archival metadata is confirmed, the image combines Morocco, a Fez craftsman, zellij and a public technical demonstration. This documents living craft knowledge, not only decoration.

Dar Mustapha Pasha, Algiers

The archive illustrates a methodological rule: the presence of tiles in a building does not prove local origin. Some tiles are explicitly identified as imported, including Delft ware. Place of use must be distinguished from place of production and technical origin.

Musée du Louvre — Morocco, 1300–1500

The Louvre exhibition “Le Maroc médiéval – Un empire de l’Afrique à l’Espagne”, held from 17 October 2014 to 19 January 2015, presented the eleventh to fifteenth centuries through the Almoravid, Almohad and Marinid dynasties centred on Morocco, whose political and artistic reach extended into northern Algeria and Tunisia.

In the Department of Islamic Art, display case 56B is titled “Maroc, 1300–1500 – Le décor architectural”. It includes ceramic mosaic elements discovered at the Sidi Boumediene Mosque in Tlemcen, including objects 74.1962.518 and 74.1962.520.

Charles Thomas-Stanford, in About Algeria (1912), describes Mansourah as the city built by Abu Yaqub, king of Fez, during the siege of Tlemcen. When discussing Sidi Boumediene, he notes tiles that were said to be partly of Moroccan origin.

Cook’s Practical Guide to Algeria and Tunisia (1908) states of the Sidi Boumediene Mosque: “The Mosque is rich in tile mosaics, some of which are said to have been made in Morocco.” The same guide attributes the neighbouring madrasa to the Marinid Abu al-Hasan and Mansourah to Abu Yaqub.

Fez and living heritage: from city to workshop

Workshop continuity

Under the logic of the 2003 Convention, the decisive factor is the human chain still practising the technique: masters, apprentices, kilns, pigments, cutting, tools, templates, geometric calculation and assembly.

Fez and UNESCO since 1980

International safeguarding campaigns for the medina of Fez have documented the urban fabric and traditional crafts since the 1970s, showing that the Fez-zellij association predates the current dispute by decades.

“Dar Zellij” and craft institutions

Workshops and institutions explicitly linked to zellij show an organized economic and cultural practice. The evidentiary value lies in the skills maintained inside them, not merely in the commercial name.

Contemporary international projects

The use of Fez-made zellij in modern projects abroad shows a craft that is still produced, exported and integrated into contemporary architecture.

Contemporary recognition and protection

Adidas dispute, 2022

Following an official Moroccan protest and discussions with Morocco’s Ministry of Culture, Adidas announced on 14 October 2022 a “positive resolution” of the dispute and acknowledged that the Algerian jersey design was “indeed inspired by the Zellige mosaics pattern”. The company also expressed deep respect for the people of Morocco and its craftsmen and regretted the controversy.

“Zellige de Fès” — 2016

The international registration shows that Morocco has for years regulated and protected a designation and commercial identity linked to Fez zellij. It does not mean WIPO ruled that all zellij worldwide belongs to Morocco.

Strongest synthesis: the argument is not that every historic use of glazed ceramic outside Morocco is inauthentic, but that glazed ceramic must be distinguished from cut-and-assembled zellij as a technical and artisanal system. Almohad and Marinid architecture, European archives and the continuity of workshops in Fez, Meknes and Marrakech document Morocco as a major historical centre for the maturation of this technique and the continuous transmission of its craft knowledge.

UNESCO conformity test: criteria R.1–R.5

Procedural rule: for the Representative List, the submitting State must demonstrate that the element satisfies all criteria R.1 to R.5. The mere presence of a nomination form, photographs, consent documents or an inventory extract does not by itself establish substantive conformity. Official UNESCO criteria.

R.1 — A living, identifiable and precisely defined element

This is the principal vulnerability. File 02280 groups under “zellidje, glazed ceramics” Hammadid tesserae from Béjaïa, medieval decoration in Tlemcen and contemporary practice in Tipasa. Ancient architectural ceramics do not by themselves prove that these materials belong to the same living element or to one transmitted technical chain.

R.2 — Visibility, dialogue and cultural diversity

R.2 requires inscription to encourage dialogue and reflect cultural diversity. Where zellij is documented across several parts of the Maghreb and al-Andalus, the nomination should distinguish local forms and acknowledge the shared regional context rather than imply exclusivity over a broad regional concept.

R.3 — Safeguarding a living practice

Safeguarding measures should protect transmission through masters, apprentices, workshops, gestures, geometric knowledge and the production chain. Monument restoration or commercial promotion alone is not equivalent to safeguarding living intangible heritage.

R.4 — Community participation and consent

UNESCO's portal indicates that community consents were submitted, so R.4 should not be described as absent. The relevant question is qualitative: do the signatories represent the diversity of practitioners, and did communities participate in defining the element itself?

R.5 — Consistency with the national inventory

An inventory extract is present, but the name, technical definition, bearer communities and geographical scope in the inventory should correspond to the element nominated as “zellidje”. If the inventory covers a broader glazed-ceramics category, that correspondence requires scrutiny.

Procedural conclusion: if the file does not coherently demonstrate that Hammadid tesserae, medieval Tlemcen ensembles and contemporary practice constitute one precisely defined living element called zellidje, non-inscription in its current form would follow the logic of the criteria. If the gaps are considered remediable, referral for clarification would allow a more precise definition, proof of technical continuity and fuller recognition of the shared regional context.
Additional request to the Committee — defer a final decision: given the remaining ambiguities concerning the technical definition of the element, the continuity between Hammadid evidence, medieval Tlemcen ensembles and contemporary practice, and the consistency between the nominated scope and the national inventory, the Committee is requested not to take a final inscription decision at this stage. The most appropriate procedural course would be to refer the nomination to the submitting State for additional information concerning the operational chain, intergenerational transmission, bearer communities, technical terminology and the precise scope of the element. A final decision should be taken only after those clarifications have been examined.

Modern protection

The “Zellige de Fès” certification mark protects provenance and manufacturing standards; it is not an international ruling of exclusive ownership over all zellij.
