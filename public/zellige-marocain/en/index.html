Moroccan Zellige: Historical, Textual and Material Evidence

A documentary dossier bringing together historical testimony, written sources and material evidence to highlight the authenticity of Moroccan zellige and its development across the centuries.

Introduction

Before becoming a major feature of contemporary architecture and design, zellige developed through a long history of clay, fire, glazing, geometry and manual skill. In Fez, Marrakesh, Meknes and other Moroccan cities, the tradition appears through a cumulative chain of evidence: historical texts, dated monuments, craft techniques, independent archives and a living practice.

The central question is therefore not simply where the earliest glazed ceramic was found, but where a complete technical and craft sequence can be traced: raw material, firing, glazing, cutting, geometric assembly, transmission from master to apprentice, and continuity from the historical workshop to the present-day craft.

This study brings medieval texts, archaeological data and material evidence into dialogue with living practice. It distinguishes different forms of architectural ceramics and follows the development of Moroccan zellige toward its mature Marinid form while situating it within earlier traditions of paving and ceramic revetment.

All zellige belongs to the field of ceramics, but not all ceramics are zellige.

This distinction allows tesserae, tiles, mosaics, pavements and cut-and-assembled zellige to be compared with technical precision rather than treated as interchangeable categories.

Aghmat • 11th–12th centuries • Almoravid period

Aghmat in the Almoravid period: an imperial capital and an early centre of architectural ceramics

The excavations at Aghmat, about 30 km south of Marrakesh, provide an important archaeological sequence for understanding the formation of Moroccan traditions of glazed ceramics, paving and architectural decoration before their later Almohad and Marinid development.

The Aghmat Archaeological Mission notes that the city was already flourishing in the Idrisid period and became an imperial capital when the Almoravids took control in the eleventh century. Archaeological research places the main development phase of the monumental quarter in the eleventh and twelfth centuries, precisely the period in which Aghmat was an Almoravid political and economic centre.

Excavations have uncovered a large bathhouse, palace complex, mosque, elite residences, hydraulic systems, pools, carved stucco and geometric decoration. Archaeology Magazine also reports substantial quantities of glazed cuerda seca pottery. Unfinished and defective pieces led researchers to consider local production, indicating a resident craft community with advanced knowledge of forming, glazing and firing ceramics.

Decorative pavements, pools and architectural revetments around the mosque and residential areas place Aghmat within an early Moroccan architectural environment in which ceramic technology, geometry, water and decoration were closely connected.

An Almoravid capital

Aghmat became an Almoravid political centre in the eleventh century before the foundation of Marrakesh, giving special significance to its craft and architectural remains.

Glazed ceramics and local production

The cuerda seca finds, including unfinished and misfired pieces, support the presence of local ceramic production and specialised craftspeople.

Architectural decoration

Palace, mosque, pools, stucco and decorated floors reveal a sophisticated built environment in which ornamental and geometric techniques were already highly developed.

Long continuity at the site

Aghmat remained occupied after the political centre shifted to Marrakesh. Fourteenth-century excavations later document polychrome zellige tiles preserved in situ in an elite residence.

Contribution to the dossier: Aghmat documents an early Moroccan craft and architectural base for glazed ceramics, paving and geometric decoration during the Almoravid period. Read together with Almohad evidence from Marrakesh and Marinid evidence from Fez, it provides a dated archaeological sequence for the development of Moroccan ceramic revetment traditions.

From bejmat to cut zellij: a documented technical evolution

Al-Qarawiyyin in 526 H / 1131

Ibn Abi Zar al-Fasi records the repaving of al-Qarawiyyin's courtyard in Rawd al-Qirtas. He names the master Muhammad b. Ahmad b. Muhammad al-Khawlani and mentions the manufacture of “brick resembling bejmat”, together with a detailed account of levelling and water drainage. The text also states that an earlier pavement had existed.

Significance: this is early written evidence for ceramic paving in Fez before Marinid maturity.

Functional evolution

A cautious sequence can be proposed: large paving units / bejmat → wider glazed-tile use → zellij in floors and wall dados → small cut pieces assembled geometrically in the Marinid period. Al-Umari's testimony is central because he describes zellij in Fez floors and wall dados.

Ceuta in 825 H

Muhammad b. al-Qasim al-Subti explicitly describes a basin “paved with coloured zellij”, providing direct Moroccan textual evidence from the early fifteenth century.

View source →

Qal'a of the Beni Hammad: compare techniques, not labels

Museum With No Frontiers describes a Hammadid panel made of moulded, enamelled and lustre-painted ceramic; its stars and crosses are about 21 cm in diameter. These are large moulded elements, not necessarily small units created by post-firing cutting.

By contrast, the ArchéoSciences study of fourteenth-century Moroccan zellij describes a process in which tiles are glazed and fired, then cut and finally assembled into geometric panels.

Fez raw material: what science actually establishes

A recent study of Fez clays shows that potters traditionally use two clay types from the Benjlik site, fine and sandy clays from Miocene deposits in the Fez basin. Their mineralogical and chemical composition and their ceramic suitability have been scientifically characterized.

Supported claim: Fez has a scientifically studied local raw material traditionally tied to pottery and zellij production.

Tlemcen: historical and technical comparison

Methodological note : Modern borders inherited from colonial rule should not be projected onto earlier centuries. Attribution should be based on chronology, patronage, technique, written sources and the political context of each monument.

From Moroccan craftsmen to Marinid influence on Nasrid architecture

Historical sources and archaeological studies show that relations between Morocco and al-Andalus involved real movements of craftsmen, master builders and technical knowledge.

For the construction of Seville's great mosque in 567 H / 1172, Ibn Sahib al-Salat records the participation of master builders from Marrakesh, Fez and the Maghribi shore alongside Andalusi craftsmen.

In the sixteenth century, Luis del Mármol Carvajal states that the rulers of Granada acted « a imitación de los reyes de Fez » and adds that « los reyes de Granada siempre fueron imitando a los de Fez ».

Antonio Malpica Cuello points to « interesantes paralelos » between Marinid and Nasrid ceramics and describes them as « estrechamente ligadas ». Research by the Patronato de la Alhambra further notes that a precise ceramic-inlay technique is documented, as far as currently known, only in Nasrid and Marinid art.

Conclusion: a documented line can be traced from Almohad-era movement of Moroccan craftsmen to Fez/Marinid influence in Granada and direct technical affinities between Marinid and Nasrid ceramics. For zellij specifically, this establishes a strong historical and technical environment for transfer and influence without proving a one-way transfer of every technique.

Independent external documentation before the modern dispute

Albert Kahn archive — Fez and Meknes, 1913

Photographs of urban spaces and fountains document zellij in everyday city life, not merely as a museum object, long before current heritage disputes.

Paris Colonial Exhibition, 1931

If the archival metadata is confirmed, the image combines Morocco, a Fez craftsman, zellij and a public technical demonstration. This documents living craft knowledge, not only decoration.

Dar Mustapha Pasha, Algiers

The archive illustrates a methodological rule: the presence of tiles in a building does not prove local origin. Some tiles are explicitly identified as imported, including Delft ware. Place of use must be distinguished from place of production and technical origin.

Musée du Louvre — Morocco, 1300–1500

The Louvre exhibition “Le Maroc médiéval – Un empire de l’Afrique à l’Espagne”, held from 17 October 2014 to 19 January 2015, presented the eleventh to fifteenth centuries through the Almoravid, Almohad and Marinid dynasties centred on Morocco, whose political and artistic reach extended into northern Algeria and Tunisia.

In the Department of Islamic Art, display case 56B is titled “Maroc, 1300–1500 – Le décor architectural”. It includes ceramic mosaic elements discovered at the Sidi Boumediene Mosque in Tlemcen, including objects 74.1962.518 and 74.1962.520.

Charles Thomas-Stanford, in About Algeria (1912), describes Mansourah as the city built by Abu Yaqub, king of Fez, during the siege of Tlemcen. When discussing Sidi Boumediene, he notes tiles that were said to be partly of Moroccan origin.

Cook’s Practical Guide to Algeria and Tunisia (1908) states of the Sidi Boumediene Mosque: “The Mosque is rich in tile mosaics, some of which are said to have been made in Morocco.” The same guide attributes the neighbouring madrasa to the Marinid Abu al-Hasan and Mansourah to Abu Yaqub.

Fez and living heritage: from city to workshop

Workshop continuity

Under the logic of the 2003 Convention, the decisive factor is the human chain still practising the technique: masters, apprentices, kilns, pigments, cutting, tools, templates, geometric calculation and assembly.

Fez and UNESCO since 1980

International safeguarding campaigns for the medina of Fez have documented the urban fabric and traditional crafts since the 1970s, showing that the Fez-zellij association predates the current dispute by decades.

“Dar Zellij” and craft institutions

Workshops and institutions explicitly linked to zellij show an organized economic and cultural practice. The evidentiary value lies in the skills maintained inside them, not merely in the commercial name.

Contemporary international projects

The use of Fez-made zellij in modern projects abroad shows a craft that is still produced, exported and integrated into contemporary architecture.

Contemporary recognition and protection

Adidas dispute, 2022

Following an official Moroccan protest and discussions with Morocco’s Ministry of Culture, Adidas announced on 14 October 2022 a “positive resolution” of the dispute and acknowledged that the Algerian jersey design was “indeed inspired by the Zellige mosaics pattern”. The company also expressed deep respect for the people of Morocco and its craftsmen and regretted the controversy.

“Zellige de Fès” — 2016

The international registration shows that Morocco has for years regulated and protected a designation and commercial identity linked to Fez zellij. It does not mean WIPO ruled that all zellij worldwide belongs to Morocco.

Strongest synthesis: the argument is not that every historic use of glazed ceramic outside Morocco is inauthentic, but that glazed ceramic must be distinguished from cut-and-assembled zellij as a technical and artisanal system. Almohad and Marinid architecture, European archives and the continuity of workshops in Fez, Meknes and Marrakech document Morocco as a major historical centre for the maturation of this technique and the continuous transmission of its craft knowledge.

UNESCO conformity test: criteria R.1–R.5

Procedural rule: for the Representative List, the submitting State must demonstrate that the element satisfies all criteria R.1 to R.5. The mere presence of a nomination form, photographs, consent documents or an inventory extract does not by itself establish substantive conformity. Official UNESCO criteria.

R.1 — A living, identifiable and precisely defined element

This is the principal vulnerability. File 02280 groups under “zellidje, glazed ceramics” Hammadid tesserae from Béjaïa, medieval decoration in Tlemcen and contemporary practice in Tipasa. Ancient architectural ceramics do not by themselves prove that these materials belong to the same living element or to one transmitted technical chain.

R.2 — Visibility, dialogue and cultural diversity

R.2 requires inscription to encourage dialogue and reflect cultural diversity. Where zellij is documented across several parts of the Maghreb and al-Andalus, the nomination should distinguish local forms and acknowledge the shared regional context rather than imply exclusivity over a broad regional concept.

R.3 — Safeguarding a living practice

Safeguarding measures should protect transmission through masters, apprentices, workshops, gestures, geometric knowledge and the production chain. Monument restoration or commercial promotion alone is not equivalent to safeguarding living intangible heritage.

R.4 — Community participation and consent

UNESCO's portal indicates that community consents were submitted, so R.4 should not be described as absent. The relevant question is qualitative: do the signatories represent the diversity of practitioners, and did communities participate in defining the element itself?

R.5 — Consistency with the national inventory

An inventory extract is present, but the name, technical definition, bearer communities and geographical scope in the inventory should correspond to the element nominated as “zellidje”. If the inventory covers a broader glazed-ceramics category, that correspondence requires scrutiny.

Procedural conclusion: if the file does not coherently demonstrate that Hammadid tesserae, medieval Tlemcen ensembles and contemporary practice constitute one precisely defined living element called zellidje, non-inscription in its current form would follow the logic of the criteria. If the gaps are considered remediable, referral for clarification would allow a more precise definition, proof of technical continuity and fuller recognition of the shared regional context.
Additional request to the Committee — defer a final decision: given the remaining ambiguities concerning the technical definition of the element, the continuity between Hammadid evidence, medieval Tlemcen ensembles and contemporary practice, and the consistency between the nominated scope and the national inventory, the Committee is requested not to take a final inscription decision at this stage. The most appropriate procedural course would be to refer the nomination to the submitting State for additional information concerning the operational chain, intergenerational transmission, bearer communities, technical terminology and the precise scope of the element. A final decision should be taken only after those clarifications have been examined.

Modern protection

The “Zellige de Fès” certification mark protects provenance and manufacturing standards; it is not an international ruling of exclusive ownership over all zellij.

Conclusion and recommendation

After examining the historical, archaeological and technical evidence underlying Algerian nomination 02280, and comparing it with specialist definitions of zellige and the requirements of the 2003 Convention, the central question is whether the nomination demonstrates that the various pieces of evidence grouped under “zellidje” constitute one living element with a technically and historically continuous chain of transmission.

The nomination brings together ancient tesserae and ceramics, medieval decoration from Tlemcen and contemporary practice. Chronological succession alone does not necessarily establish technical and craft continuity. The Committee should therefore examine continuity in production methods, tools, terminology, practitioner communities and modes of transmission.

Under R.1, the element should be defined precisely enough to distinguish it from the wider field of glazed architectural ceramics. R.4 and R.5 likewise require careful examination of community participation and of the correspondence between the nominated element and the national inventory entry.

The Moroccan comparison shows a documentary chain linking dated medieval monuments, written sources, independent archives, workshops, masters, apprentices and living transmission. This is not a request for UNESCO to award exclusive cultural ownership; it is a call for a technically and historically precise definition of the nominated element.

Accordingly, if the Committee considers that the element’s definition and its technical and historical continuity have not been sufficiently demonstrated, nomination 02280 should not be inscribed in its current form.

At a minimum, if the shortcomings are considered remediable, referral should be considered so that the title and scope can be clarified, glazed ceramics, tiles, mosaics and cut-and-assembled zellige can be clearly distinguished, and stronger evidence of continuity between historical evidence and contemporary practice can be supplied.

Such a decision would not be a position against Algeria; it would protect the scientific precision of the Representative List and the credibility of the 2003 Convention system.

Documentary review for Committee members: Algerian nomination 02280 and draft decision 21.COM 7.b.32

Independent review for members of the Intergovernmental Committee for the Safeguarding of the Intangible Cultural Heritage at its 21st session (2026). It compares the historical and technical evidence in nomination ICH-02 with criteria R.1–R.5. This is not an official UNESCO document.

1. Qal'a of the Beni Hammad and the definition of the element — R.1

The nomination cites eleventh-century ceramic remains to support the practice’s historical presence. These demonstrate architectural ceramic decoration, but geometric resemblance alone does not establish identical forming, cutting, glazing and assembly techniques to those used today. Material analysis would need to identify cutting before or after firing, tool marks and fixing methods. Criterion R.1 does not award exclusive origin or archaeological precedence; it concerns living heritage recognized by its communities.

2. El Mechouar Palace: original work versus modern reconstruction

The UNESCO photograph is expressly captioned as showing the fountain after restoration. Zahira Seddiki’s thesis (Constantine 3 University) describes 283.65 square metres of historic zellige and 1,300 square metres of recently restored zellige: approximately 17.9% and 82.1% of those two figures combined, not of the entire palace. Press reports mention Moroccan artisans taking part in works preceding the 2011 inauguration, but contracts or work records are needed to attribute specific surfaces. The date and makers of the tiles visible in the nominated photograph should be distinguished from the date of the monument.

3. Tipaza workshops: documenting the complete craft

Official captions describe shaping an eight-pointed clay star, assembling unglazed pieces, and applying glaze by brush. These are ceramic operations, but do not on their own document the whole production chain. The Fez technique includes cutting glazed tiles after firing, while other historic Moroccan methods have involved cutting before firing. The workshop’s tools, finished products and full film should therefore be matched to the element’s stated definition without treating one regional technique as universal.

4. Transmission and safeguarding — R.1 and R.3

The nomination describes family workshops, vocational training and international experts. These can coexist, but the pathways should be documented by identifying the communities, the skills transmitted and the training content. General aid for ceramists does not automatically show dedicated safeguards for geometric planning, tile cutting and mosaic assembly. Measures, implementers, budgets and monitoring indicators need to be distinguishable.

5. Community participation and inventory — R.4 and R.5

The nomination names the Ayadi El Djazaïr association and other craftspeople and cites inventory entry 2023/A/03 dated 24 October 2023. UNESCO lists consent and inventory attachments, so their existence is not in dispute. The question is whether their contents establish informed participation and consistent definitions of the element.

6. Draft decision and paragraph 4

Paragraph 3 proposes inscription; it is not yet a final adopted decision. Paragraph 4 stresses that inscription confers neither an exclusive origin nor ownership or intellectual-property rights in intangible heritage. This legal limit does not decide the craft’s historical origin or excuse inaccurate supporting documentation. Algeria holds a vice-presidency of the Committee Bureau, but that office alone does not establish improper pressure.

7. Possible referral for additional information

The Operational Directives allow referral when information is insufficient to assess one or more criteria. A dispute over historical priority alone is not enough. Any request for clarification should identify a concrete evidentiary gap tied to R.1–R.5 and establish whether the evaluation report, full film or attached records already address it.

Documentary conclusion

The Moroccan dossier assembles textual, architectural and technical sources, including Aghmat, Fez and UNESCO’s 1978 record of the Nejjarine Fountain. The Algerian examples raise testable questions about the Hammadid fragments’ technical relationship to present practice, the dating of restored El Mechouar decoration and the Tipaza demonstrations. These questions should not be represented as a final finding on eligibility without examining the complete official record.
